3a. Standard, configure, customize, or buy

ChoiceWhat it meansOngoing effect
StandardUse the vendor's built-in workflow as is.Easiest to upgrade and support; staff may need to change habits.
ConfigureChange settings the vendor designed to be changed (templates, rules, banners, privacy limits).Usually included in the price; survives upgrades.
Custom buildBuild something the vendor doesn't offer, or change how the product works.Costs money every year; must be retested and often rebuilt at each upgrade.
Add-on productBuy an extra module or partner product.A yearly fee, another contract, and another interface to support.

Many health systems try to stay as close to the vendor's standard setup as they can. One chief medical information officer explained in 2026 that customization creates long-term maintenance burdens and makes it harder to adopt the vendor's future improvements (Becker's Hospital Review, July 7, 2026). A good rule: configure before you customize, and customize only for patient safety, legal requirements, or a clear, measured benefit.

Practice question (not graded)

Request R04 asks for a custom pediatric dosing calculator ($60,000 one time, $9,000 a year). The new EHR has standard weight-based dosing with pediatric rules. What is the best first step?

3b. The five-year cost

A request's price tag is not its cost. Add the yearly cost for every year you will use it: five-year cost = one-time + 5 × yearly. Small yearly fees add up, and AI add-ons are often priced per user per month. For example, reports in 2025 put ambient AI scribes at about $200 to $600 per physician per month (Becker's Hospital Review, May 27, 2025).

Practice question (not graded)

Request R02: an ambient AI scribe for 40 physicians, $30,000 one time and $192,000 a year. What is its five-year cost?

3c. AI features need local proof and oversight

AI features are sold with accuracy numbers measured somewhere else. A well-known study tested a widely used vendor sepsis prediction model on about 40,000 hospital stays at one academic medical center and found it sorted patients correctly far less often than the vendor reported (63% versus 76% to 83%). It alerted on nearly 1 in 5 patients, and only about 1 in 8 alerted patients had sepsis (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2021; Michigan Medicine). The lesson for managers: validate AI on your own patients before you rely on it, watch for alert fatigue, keep a human in charge of every decision, and monitor performance and fairness after go-live. The ACHE Code (Section III, item 14) now asks leaders to set ethics frameworks for buying, implementing, and monitoring AI.

Practice question (not graded)

A vendor says its AI alert is 80% accurate. What should Kestrel Falls do before turning it on for every unit?

3d. Saying no well

Most requests come from a real need. A "no" to a custom build should come with a better way to meet the need: a configured template, training, a later phase, or a measured trial. That is how you keep buy-in from Module 5 while protecting the budget and the upgrade path.

Step 4 · Copilot practice · 60 min

This week's technique: provide examples (few-shot prompting)

When you want Copilot to sort many items the way you would, show it a few worked examples first: the item, your decision, and your reason. This is called few-shot prompting (asking with no examples is zero-shot, which you used in Module 1). Examples teach the pattern better than a description, but Copilot will copy them closely, so pick examples that show different decisions, and check every result.

Sources: Microsoft Learn, "Write prompts that get results" (updated October 2026); Anthropic, "Prompt engineering best practices for 2026" (published November 10, 2025); IBM, "The 2026 Guide to Prompt Engineering" (published July 23, 2025). You write your own prompts in this course; none are provided to copy.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

A clinic operations manager had 30 requests for new appointment types. She wrote two examples into her prompt: "Annual wellness visit, 40 minutes: keep, because Medicare covers it and it needs a set length," and "Quick question, 5 minutes: merge into 'brief visit,' because it duplicates an existing type." Then she asked Copilot to sort the other 28 the same way, with a reason for each. Copilot sorted most of them sensibly but merged two that her examples didn't cover well, including a pediatric visit that needed its own length. She fixed those two and added a third example for next time.
