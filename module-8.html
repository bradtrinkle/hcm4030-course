Welcome to Module 8. Word got out that Kestrel Falls Community Hospital is choosing a new EHR, and now every department wants something. Sixteen requests came in, from a custom operative note for Dr. Halvorsen to three AI features. The board set aside a change reserve of $2.4 million over five years. By April 4, Laura needs a customization decision log: a decision and a reason for every request, the five-year cost, and how the hospital will govern the AI features.

In this video I’ll cover the four choices for any request, why experienced leaders stay close to standard, how to figure a five-year cost, why AI features need local proof and oversight, and how to say no in a way that keeps people on your side.

Every request lands in one of four places. Standard means using the vendor’s built-in workflow as is. It’s the easiest to upgrade and support, though staff may need to change habits.

Configure means changing settings the vendor designed to be changed, like templates, rules, banners, and privacy limits. It’s usually included in the price, and it survives upgrades.

Custom build means building something the vendor doesn’t offer, or changing how the product works. It costs money every year, and it has to be retested, and often rebuilt, at every upgrade.

And an add-on product means buying an extra module or a partner’s product. That brings a yearly fee, another contract, and another interface to support.

The AMA’s transition guide makes this its own step, deciding on customization, because these choices shape the system for years.

Many health systems try to stay as close to the vendor’s standard setup as they can. In July 2026, the chief medical information officer at John Muir Health explained why. She said customization creates long-term maintenance burdens and makes it harder to adopt the vendor’s future improvements. Her system reviews the vendor’s product roadmap every year and sends requests through governance committees, with executives setting priorities.

There’s a second reason. A new EHR is a chance to fix old workflows. A request to make the new screen look exactly like the old one copies old habits, and sometimes old workarounds, into the new system.

So here’s a good rule: configure before you customize, and customize only for patient safety, legal requirements, or a clear, measured benefit.

A request’s price tag is not its cost. Add the yearly cost for every year you’ll use it. For this module, five-year cost equals the one-time cost plus five times the yearly cost.

Small yearly fees add up fast, and AI add-ons are often priced per user per month. Becker’s reported in 2025 that ambient AI scribes cost about $200 to $600 per physician per month. Multiply that by dozens of physicians and twelve months, and a “small” monthly fee becomes one of the biggest lines in the budget.

Then compare the total of everything you approve with the reserve. If the total is over $2.4 million, something has to be cut, deferred, or paid for another way, and your log should say what.

AI features are sold with accuracy numbers measured somewhere else. Here’s why that matters.

Researchers at Michigan Medicine tested a widely used vendor sepsis prediction model on about 38,000 hospital stays. The model performed much worse than the developer had reported. It sent alerts for 18 percent of hospitalized patients, and it still missed about two-thirds of the patients who had sepsis. So nurses got lots of alerts, most of them for patients without sepsis, and many real cases didn’t trigger one at all.

The lesson for managers isn’t “never use AI.” It’s this: validate AI on your own patients before you rely on it, often by running it silently first and comparing its alerts with what actually happened. Watch for alert fatigue. Keep a human in charge of every decision. And monitor performance and fairness after go-live, because a model can drift as patients and practices change.

Rules are catching up. Federal certification rules from ONC, known as HTI-1, now require developers of certified health IT to give customers information about the predictive tools they supply: how the tool was developed, how it performed in validation, how fairness was checked, and how it will be maintained. That gives a hospital the questions to ask. It doesn’t answer them for you.

The ACHE Code of Ethics was amended in December 2025. It now asks healthcare leaders to set ethics frameworks for how AI is bought, put in place, and monitored. In practice, a good oversight rule answers four questions. Who approves the AI feature? What is checked before go-live? What is monitored after? And who can turn it off if it isn’t working?

Most requests come from a real need. A surgeon who wants the old note screen is worried about speed and safety in a high-pressure job. A pharmacy that wants to keep its calculator is worried about children’s doses.

So a no to a custom build should come with a better way to meet the need. That might be a configured template, extra training, a place in a later phase, or a measured trial with a date to decide.

This is how you keep the buy-in you built in Module 5 while protecting the budget and the upgrade path. A flat no turns a skeptic into an opponent. A no with a real alternative often turns them into a partner.

Let’s work a made-up request, not one of Kestrel Falls Community Hospital’s sixteen. The lab asks for a custom specimen-label screen: $40,000 one time and $18,000 a year. The new EHR has a standard label screen that can be configured with the lab’s own fields.

First, the five-year cost: $40,000 plus five times $18,000, which is $90,000. That’s $130,000 in total.

Second, the decision. The standard screen can be configured to show the fields the lab needs, so the first step is configure, not build. Decision: configure, and test it with the lab before go-live. If a real safety gap remains, like a field that can’t be added, come back to a custom build with evidence.

Third, the reply: “We’re not approving a custom screen, but we will configure the standard one with your fields and test it with your team in June. If it misses something that affects patient safety, we’ll revisit.”

This week’s technique is few-shot prompting. You give Copilot a couple of worked examples, like that one, and ask it to handle the rest the same way. Examples steer AI strongly, so pick two of your own decisions that are different from each other. Then check every row. Copilot may copy your examples too closely, or get a cost wrong.

Step 4 · Copilot practice · 60 min

This week's technique: provide examples (few-shot prompting)

When you want Copilot to sort many items the way you would, show it a few worked examples first: the item, your decision, and your reason. This is called few-shot prompting (asking with no examples is zero-shot, which you used in Module 1). Examples teach the pattern better than a description, but Copilot will copy them closely, so pick examples that show different decisions, and check every result.

Sources: Microsoft Learn, "Write prompts that get results" (updated October 2026); Anthropic, "Prompt engineering best practices for 2026" (published November 10, 2025); IBM, "The 2026 Guide to Prompt Engineering" (published July 23, 2025). You write your own prompts in this course; none are provided to copy.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

A clinic operations manager had 30 requests for new appointment types. She wrote two examples into her prompt: "Annual wellness visit, 40 minutes: keep, because Medicare covers it and it needs a set length," and "Quick question, 5 minutes: merge into 'brief visit,' because it duplicates an existing type." Then she asked Copilot to sort the other 28 the same way, with a reason for each. Copilot sorted most of them sensibly but merged two that her examples didn't cover well, including a pediatric visit that needed its own length. She fixed those two and added a third example for next time.
