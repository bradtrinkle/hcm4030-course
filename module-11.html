Welcome to Module 11. Kestrel Falls Community Hospital’s April pulse survey is back, and burnout is up in every group since October. Nurses are short-staffed before training has even started. The help desk is down two people. And the transition team is doing this project on top of their regular jobs. Greg has given Laura $600,000 for the year around go-live to reduce that stress, and HR’s menu of ideas adds up to about three times that. By April 25, Laura needs a stress-reduction plan.

In this video I’ll cover how tired the healthcare workforce already is, why a transition adds load, what kinds of fixes work best, how to read a pulse survey, and how the same plan looks to a manager, an executive, and a board.

In 2022, the U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory calling health worker burnout a crisis. Things have improved some. In the American Medical Association’s survey of nearly 18,000 physicians, 43.2 percent reported burnout in 2024, down from 48.2 percent in 2023 and 53 percent in 2022. More than a third said ineffective EHR systems, inbox work, and documentation took time away from patient care. Nurses tell a similar story. In the National Council of State Boards of Nursing’s 2024 study, nearly 40 percent of registered nurses said they intend to leave the field within five years. More than 138,000 nurses had left since 2022. So Kestrel Falls Community Hospital isn’t starting from rest. It’s starting tired.

An EHR change adds work before it saves any. People train on top of full shifts. Super users and the transition team go to design meetings. Everyone practices in the new system. And after go-live, everything takes longer for a while. You saw in Module 6 that the productivity dip lasts months, not days.

Staff who were already short-handed feel it most. In Kestrel Falls Community Hospital’s survey, the inpatient nurses’ most-named stressor is short staffing and mandatory overtime, and their burnout rose from 46 to 52 percent in six months.

People rarely burn out from one hard week. They burn out when the load never lets up, and nobody seems to notice.

What actually helps? A meta-analysis of controlled studies of physicians found that interventions did reduce burnout, and that the effect was greater when they were aimed at the organization, things like workload, schedules, workflow, and teamwork, than when they were aimed at the individual. That’s the difference between a wellness app and a float nurse. One comment in the Kestrel Falls Community Hospital survey says it well: free pizza is nice; a float nurse would be nicer.

Individual support still has a place. Peer support after a hard shift matters. But it can’t fix a short-staffed unit, or an inbox that follows a doctor home every night.

A pulse survey is a short, frequent check on how staff are doing. Read it four ways.

Look at the level: how high is burnout in each group? Look at the trend: which groups got worse since the last survey, and by how much? Check who answered: ten of ten help desk staff is a full picture; a third of a group may not be. And read what people said. The numbers tell you where; the comments tell you why.

Then match causes to fixes. If a group’s top stressor is short staffing, an app won’t move its number. If it’s abusive patients at the front desk, the fix is safety, not a class.

The same plan looks different from different chairs.

A unit manager asks: will my staff feel this on their next shift? Will I get a float nurse, or a memo?

The executive team asks: what does it cost, what does it protect, and what waits? Losing nurses before go-live is expensive. So is a go-live where tired staff make mistakes.

The board asks: is the hospital meeting its duty to its workforce and its patients, and is the money well spent?

A strong plan answers all three. And it uses the free changes too, like pausing projects that aren’t essential, keeping vacation blackouts short, and making overtime voluntary first.

The ACHE Code of Ethics lists leaders’ responsibilities to employees. They include encouraging people to raise concerns, a healthy workplace free of harassment and coercion, a safe and equitable work environment, preventing violence, and fair pay for the work performed. Under pressure, go-live memos can drift the other way: no vacations for months, unlimited mandatory overtime, survey answers shown to managers, front-desk staff told to handle abusive patients alone. Each one might seem to protect the project. Each one adds to the stress you’re trying to reduce, and each one tests the Code.

Let’s work a made-up case, not Kestrel Falls Community Hospital. Imagine a clinic manager who asks an AI tool for ways to cut patient wait times. A first answer is often broad, like a generic list of ideas.

She doesn’t start over. She follows up. Follow-up one: use only the data I attached; our longest waits are Monday mornings. Follow-up two: which of these cost under $5,000? Follow-up three: write those as a note to my physicians, under 100 words.

Each follow-up should bring the answer closer to what she needs. And she still checks the Monday numbers herself before she sends anything.

That’s this week’s technique: refine with follow-up prompts. Treat the first answer as a draft. Ask Copilot to narrow it to the survey’s biggest stressors, make it fit $600,000 with the arithmetic shown, and reshape it for one audience. Then check every number.

2a. How tired the workforce already is

In 2022, the U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory calling health worker burnout a crisis, citing reports of burnout in up to 54% of nurses and physicians (HHS, May 23, 2022). Things have improved some since then, but not enough. In the American Medical Association's survey of nearly 18,000 physicians, 43.2% reported burnout in 2024, down from 48.2% in 2023 and 53% in 2022; more than a third said ineffective EHR systems, inbox work, and documentation took time from patient care (AMA, May 1, 2025). In the National Council of State Boards of Nursing's 2024 study, nearly 40% of registered nurses said they intend to leave the field within five years, and more than 138,000 nurses had left since 2022 (Becker's Hospital Review, April 17, 2025).

2b. A transition adds load

An EHR change adds work before it saves any: training on top of full shifts, design meetings, practice time, double-checking, and a productivity dip that lasts months (Module 6). Staff who were already short-handed feel it most. People don't burn out from one hard week; they burn out when the load never lets up and nobody seems to notice.

2c. Fix the work, not just the worker

A meta-analysis of 19 comparisons from 18 controlled studies of physicians found that interventions reduced burnout, and that the effect was greater when interventions were directed at the organization (workload, schedules, workflow, teamwork) than at the individual physician (Panagioti et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2017). A wellness app or a resilience class can help, but it can't fix a short-staffed unit or an inbox that follows a doctor home.

The board asks: is the hospital meeting its duty to its workforce and patients, and is the money well spent? A strong plan answers all three.
