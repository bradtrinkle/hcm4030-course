2a. How hospitals choose an EHR vendor

A typical process: write down what the hospital needs, send a request for proposals (RFP), watch scripted demonstrations where vendors show real workflows, call and visit reference sites similar to your hospital, score each vendor against weighted criteria, and only then negotiate the contract. Skipping steps, especially reference checks, is how hospitals end up with surprises.

2b. Total cost of ownership

A quote shows what the vendor charges. Total cost of ownership adds everything else the hospital will pay over the same period: hardware, extra staff, price increases written into the contract, add-on products, archives for old data, and the cost of keeping the old system running longer if go-live is late. Compare vendors on the same time period (here, five years) and on the same list of costs.

Practice question (not graded)

A contract says the subscription rises 5% a year starting in year 2. Year 1 is $1.0 million. What is the five-year subscription total?

2c. Hosting models

ModelWhat it meansTrade-offs
Cloud software as a serviceThe vendor runs the system in its cloud; the hospital pays a subscription.Less hospital IT work and hardware; depends on the vendor and the Internet connection; subscription prices can rise.
Vendor-hosted (private cloud)The vendor runs the hospital's copy in its own data center.Similar to cloud; check uptime promises and what happens if service is down.
On premisesThe hospital runs the system on its own servers.More control; the hospital buys hardware and needs staff to run, patch, and secure it.

2d. Interoperability and data migration

A new EHR must connect to everything the old one did (Kestrel Falls has 38 interfaces, including the ambulance service). Ask what is included and what costs extra. Ask also how much history moves to the new system and where older records will live: patients and clinicians still need them, and law and policy set how long records must be kept.

2e. Contract terms that carry risk

  • Renewal: automatic renewals with long notice periods can lock the hospital in.
  • Price increases: escalator clauses raise costs every year.
  • Ending early: termination fees can make leaving too expensive.
  • Your data: who owns it, and in what format you get it back when the contract ends.
  • Uptime and service credits: what the vendor promises, and what you get when it fails.
  • Liability cap: the most the vendor will pay if something goes wrong.
  • Business associate agreement: under HIPAA, a vendor that handles patients' health information on the hospital's behalf must sign one. "Available on request" is not good enough for a signed contract.
Practice question (not graded)

Which contract term would most limit Kestrel Falls' ability to leave a vendor that performs poorly?

2f. Scoring and deciding

Many hospitals use a weighted scoring table: list the criteria, give each a weight (for example, cost 30%, usability 25%, risk 20%), score each vendor, and multiply. The math is only as good as the facts underneath it, so a scoring table never replaces reading the contract. The decision also depends on what can be negotiated: an escalator can be capped, an exit format can be required, a missing agreement can be made a condition of signing.

Practice question (not graded)

Tessarow goes live 22 months after an April 1, 2027 signing. What does that mean for Kestrel Falls?

Step 4 · Copilot practice · 105 min

This week's technique: specify the output format

When you compare options, the shape of the answer matters. Tell Copilot exactly what you want back: a table, which columns, which rows, what units, and what to do when information is missing (for example, "write not stated"). A set format makes gaps easy to spot and makes the answer easy to check.

Sources: Microsoft Learn, "Write prompts that get results" (updated October 2026); Anthropic, "Prompt engineering best practices for 2026" (published November 10, 2025). You write your own prompts in this course; none are provided to copy.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

A supply chain manager compared three proposals for hospital bed rentals. Her first request ("compare these three proposals") came back as three long paragraphs that were hard to line up. She asked again, naming the format: a table with one column per vendor; rows for monthly price, delivery time, service response time, contract length, and early-return fee; dollar amounts in the same units; and "not stated" where a proposal was silent. Two "not stated" cells showed her exactly what to ask the vendors next, and she checked every number in the table against the proposals.
