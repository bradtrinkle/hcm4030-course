Welcome to Module 3. The board agreed on February 16: Kestrel Falls Community Hospital will replace its EHR. Laura has to name a transition team by Friday, and her inbox is full. Some people want a seat, some want out, and some haven’t been asked but should be. She wants you to draft the team charter: who’s on the core team, what each person does, how many hours they give, and how everyone else gets a voice.

In this video I’ll cover why the team comes first, the roles a strong team needs, how super users spread a new system, how to give everyone a voice without a huge team, why time is the real budget, and what goes in a charter.

The American Medical Association’s guide, EHR Transitions: Best Practices, makes creating a transition team the very first step of an EHR change. That’s not an accident. The team makes or shapes every later decision: which vendor, how to roll out, how to train.

The federal Office of the National Coordinator for Health IT agrees. Its Health IT Playbook tells organizations to assess their readiness before they pick a system, including whether someone is available to champion the cause and whether people can work as a team. Its implementation advice starts with setting up a governance process and a project plan, and involving and communicating with staff and patients.

Here’s why it matters to a manager. If the wrong people are on the team, or the right people are missing, the mistakes don’t show up right away. They show up months later, at go-live, when they’re expensive and sometimes dangerous to fix.

Most transition teams include a similar set of roles, though hospitals name and combine them differently.

The executive sponsor owns the project for leadership. This person clears roadblocks and protects the budget and people’s time. The project manager runs the plan, the schedule, the risks, and the meetings, day to day.

A physician champion speaks for physicians and helps win their support. A nursing lead does the same for nursing. Often that’s a nurse informaticist, a nurse with training in informatics. Informatics nurses may help evaluate and select technology, define what end users need, customize the system, and design and deliver training, and they act as a bridge between clinical and technical staff. IT and technical leads cover interfaces, data, infrastructure, and security. Operations leads speak for revenue cycle, health information management, pharmacy, lab, and the clinics.

And then the role teams forget most: frontline representatives, people who do the daily work on every shift and at every site. Laura’s warning is worth repeating: a team that only looks like the leadership will miss what happens at 3 a.m.

Many frontline representatives later become super users: staff who get extra training and coach their coworkers on the unit. Researchers at Yale studied super users on two hospital units during an EHR implementation. On the unit where super users were more proactive, explained things in more depth, framed the system positively, and shared what they learned, staff became more proficient with the new system.

Two findings matter for your charter. First, on the stronger unit, super users had volunteered; on the other, managers had picked them for being tech-savvy. The authors concluded that the choice to take part predicted effectiveness better than technical skill. Second, some behaviors hurt on both units: losing patience with coworkers, spreading negative opinions about the system, and creating workarounds. So pick people who want the job, and support them.

Every group the EHR touches needs a voice. But a core team of 30 people can’t make decisions; meetings turn into speeches. Strong teams keep the core small, usually eight to ten people, and give everyone else a voice in other ways.

Workgroups handle specialty areas, like a pharmacy and lab workgroup. Super users on each unit and shift feed problems back. Meetings can be recorded for people on nights or long shifts. Regular listening sessions give anyone a place to raise concerns.

Think about the ambulance service. A paramedic on 24-hour shifts can’t come to Tuesday meetings. Leaving the crews out until go-live would be a mistake. A better design gives them a named contact, recorded meetings, and a set way to send input. Your charter should spell out how this works, not just promise it.

Here’s something that surprises new managers. The scarcest resource on a project like this usually isn’t money. It’s people’s time. Team members still have their day jobs. An hour in a project meeting is an hour away from patients, claims, or the help desk.

That’s why managers approve hours, and why leaders sometimes pay for backfill: temporary staff who cover the regular work so a key person can give real time to the project. The ONC Playbook makes a related point about implementation: plan for the training time staff need as they learn the system.

Laura gave you a hard limit: no more than 45 core-team hours a week. If your draft adds up to 52, the fix isn’t to hope people find the time. Cut hours or a seat until it fits, and say how that group still gets heard. A charter that assigns more hours than anyone has is not a plan.

A team charter is a short document, one or two pages, that everyone signs up to. It usually covers five things. The purpose: why this team exists and what it will deliver. The members and roles, with each person’s hours. How decisions are made: who decides, and what happens when the team disagrees. How often the team meets. And how others are heard.

Be specific about decisions. “The team will decide together” sounds friendly, but it stalls the first time two leaders disagree. Name who makes the final call when consensus fails, usually the executive sponsor.

And give a reason for each seat. Laura will ask why, so the reason should be in the charter.

Some people on HR’s roster asked loudly for a seat. Some are senior. Some are reluctant. Choose based on what the project needs and the evidence in the roster: approved hours, the groups they represent, and the experience they bring. Not job title alone, and not who sent the most emails. If you leave someone off the core team, say how they’ll still be heard. That’s fair to them, and it’s smart, because people support what they help build.

Let’s try a made-up example, so you can see the reasoning without me building your team. Imagine a smaller project at another hospital: a new pharmacy system. The limit is 20 core-team hours a week. The first draft has a sponsor at 2 hours, a project manager at 8, a pharmacist lead at 6, a nurse at 4, an IT analyst at 4, and a pharmacy technician from nights at 3. That’s 27 hours, seven over.

What not to do: cut the night technician because they’re the most junior. That removes the only voice from nights, the shift where errors are most likely to go unnoticed.

A better fix: the IT analyst moves to a technical workgroup that meets every other week, which saves 4 hours. The pharmacist lead drops from 6 hours to 3 because a second pharmacist covers the formulary workgroup. Now the core team is at 20 hours, and every group still has a voice. The charter says exactly that, with one sentence of reason for each change.

That’s the pattern: fit the limit, keep the coverage, and explain every trade.

2a. Why the team comes first

The American Medical Association's guide EHR Transitions: Best Practices (2024) makes creating a transition team the first step of an EHR change. The team makes or shapes every later decision: the vendor, the rollout, the training. If the wrong people are on it, or the right people are missing, the mistakes show up months later at go-live, when they are expensive to fix.

2c. Voice without a huge team

Every group the EHR touches needs a voice, but a core team of 30 cannot make decisions. Strong teams keep the core small and give others a voice through workgroups (for example, a pharmacy and lab workgroup), super users on each unit and shift, recorded meetings, and regular listening sessions. Your charter should say how this works.

2d. Time is the real budget

Team members still have their day jobs. An hour on the project is an hour away from patients, claims, or the help desk. That is why managers approve hours, and why leaders sometimes pay for backfill (temporary staff who cover the regular work). A charter that assigns more hours than anyone has is not a plan.

2e. What a team charter contains

A one- or two-page charter usually covers: the purpose, the members and roles with their time, how decisions are made (who decides, and what happens when the team disagrees), how often the team meets, and how others are heard.
