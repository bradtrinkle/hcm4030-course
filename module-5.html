3a. Why buy-in is a leadership job

An EHR is used by almost everyone in the hospital, and each group uses it differently. A surgeon writes operative notes, a CNA enters vital signs, a coder turns documentation into claims, a paramedic charts in a moving ambulance, and a patient reads results at home. If people don't understand why the change is happening, or believe it will make their work worse, they build workarounds (like writing vitals on paper and typing them in later), slow down, or wait for the project to fail. Buy-in means people understand the reason for the change, believe their concerns were heard, and are willing to try the new way. A manager earns it by listening first, involving people in decisions that affect their work, and telling the truth about what will be hard.

3b. Stakeholder mapping

A stakeholder is anyone the change affects or who can affect the change. A stakeholder map lists each one with three things: their stance (supports, mixed, neutral, worried, unsure, or opposed), their influence on whether the project succeeds (formal power, like a physician leader, or informal power, like a respected 19-year CNA), and their fears and needs in their own words. Then ask: what would move this person one step toward support? Two rules: base every entry on evidence (what they said), not on job titles; and remember that informal influence often matters more than the org chart.

Practice question (not graded)

A respected night-shift charge nurse has no formal authority over the project but is the person new nurses ask for help. Where does she belong on the map?

3c. Fears, needs, and what people don't want

Resistance usually has a reason. Listen for three things: the fear (what they think will go wrong for them or their patients), the need (what would make the change work for them), and what they don't want (often a past bad experience, like "a pamphlet that says exciting changes are coming"). When several people name the same fear, it is a common theme, and the plan must answer it directly. Treat a skeptic's objection as information: Dr. Halvorsen's worry about clicks is a usability risk the project needs to manage, not just an attitude problem.

3d. A buy-in communication plan

A communication plan answers, for each group: what they need to hear (the key message, tied to their fear or need), who says it (the messenger: people trust peers and their own leaders more than a project office), how and when (channel and timing that fit their work, such as shift huddles for nurses or early-morning meetings for surgeons), and how you will hear back (a feedback loop, so communication runs both ways). Good plans also name early adopters, people who will try the system first and tell others, and match training and support to the fears people named.

Practice question (not graded)

Evening-shift nurses say every training session is at 9 a.m. What is the best plan response?

3e. Ambient AI scribes: listening at scale

An ambient AI scribe listens to a clinic visit (with the patient's permission), turns speech into text, and drafts the visit note for the clinician to review and sign. Many hospitals are adding them to reduce documentation time. Evidence is promising but mixed. In a randomized trial of two scribe products with 238 outpatient physicians (published in NEJM AI, November 2025), one product cut time spent writing notes by about 9.5% and the other showed no clear change; physicians using either reported modest improvements in well-being, and clinically significant inaccuracies appeared "occasionally." The authors called for ongoing vigilance. The lesson for managers: these tools save effort only if a human reviews every note, and patients must be told and asked.

In Step 4 you will run the same chain on Laura's interviews: record, transcribe with AI, summarize with AI, and check the result against the source. You will see where speech-to-text slips (names, medical terms) and where a summary can overstate or miss something.

Practice question (not graded)

An ambient scribe drafts a note that says a patient "denies chest pain." The recording shows the patient said "I had some chest tightness last week." Who is responsible for catching this?

Part 2 · This week's technique: specify audience and tone (about 70 min)

The same facts land differently with different readers. When you ask Copilot to draft a message, tell it who will read it (their role, what they already know, what they care about), the tone you want (for example, direct and respectful, warm and plain, or brief and technical), and the length. Without this, Copilot writes for a generic reader in a generic upbeat voice, which is exactly the "exciting changes are coming" message staff tune out.

Sources: Microsoft Learn, "Write prompts that get results" (updated October 2026); Anthropic, "Prompt engineering best practices for 2026" (published November 10, 2025). You write your own prompts in this course; none are provided to copy.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

A clinic manager was moving her front desk to a new scheduling system. She asked Copilot to "write an email about the new scheduling system." The draft was cheerful and vague: "We're thrilled to announce an exciting upgrade!" She asked again, naming the audience (front-desk staff, most with 10 or more years on the old system, worried about long lines on day one), the tone (plain, honest, no hype), and the length (under 150 words, with the training dates first). The new draft opened with the dates, admitted the first week would be slower, and said who would be at the desk to help. She checked every date and name against her plan before sending.

Step 7 · Ethics exercise · 45 min

ACHE Code of Ethics, Section II: The Healthcare Executive's Responsibilities to Patients or Others Served

Read Section II of the ACHE Code of Ethics (as amended Dec. 8, 2025). Four items matter most here: item 2 (build trust with patients and their families), item 10 (a process that ensures patients' autonomy and self-determination), item 11 (procedures that safeguard patients' confidentiality and privacy), and item 13 (technology used in patient care decision-making tools should be transparent and equitable).

Two terms: Proxy access lets someone a patient chooses (like an adult child) use their own login to see part of the patient's portal record. Under HIPAA, an adult patient can decide what family members may see, unless a court or legal document (such as a health care power of attorney) makes someone their personal representative.
