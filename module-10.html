2a. Training that sticks

Good EHR training is role-based (a pharmacist learns pharmacy work, not everything), uses a practice system with fake patients that looks like the learner's own unit, and keeps going after go-live. In a 2026 KLAS Arch Collaborative report on more than 121,000 clinicians, clinicians asked that training not be crammed into one day or one week, but spaced over about 90 days, with accessible, ongoing education (TechTarget, July 1, 2026). Nurses in KLAS surveys have credited their EHR satisfaction to ongoing training and super-user support.

Practice question (not graded)

Which training schedule best fits what clinicians told KLAS?

2b. Training for every shift and site

Training that happens only at 9 a.m. at the main hospital misses evening and night staff, rural clinics, and crews who can't leave the road. Plans that work offer sessions at shift-friendly times (late afternoon, early morning, nights), recorded or online lessons, practice time on the unit, and training where people work, like the ambulance bases. Paying people for training time matters too: when training is on people's own time, many skip it and then struggle.

2c. Go-live support

In the first weeks, people need help in seconds, not in an hour. Plans usually combine super users (trained coworkers on every unit and shift), at-the-elbow support (extra trained staff standing on the floors), and a 24-hour command center that sorts problems by how serious they are. When Vanderbilt University Medical Center went live with a new EHR in November 2017, affecting more than 18,000 employees, it planned support at about 1 support person for every 4 providers, 1 for every 6 clinical and nonclinical staff, and 1 for every 8 in billing, on duty around the clock. End-user satisfaction with support rose from 1.90 out of 5 on day one to 3.55 within two weeks, and one lesson was that outside support staff often lacked a working knowledge of the new system (Applied Clinical Informatics, 2019). A KLAS report described a medical center that paired 2 to 4 hours of in-person training with 2 to 5 days of at-the-elbow support for new physicians and raised clinician satisfaction (TechTarget, Jan. 13, 2023).

Practice question (not graded)

At KFCH, 95 inpatient nurses are on duty at the busiest daytime hour. At a ratio of 1 support person to 6 users, how many support people do they need then?

2d. Know whether it worked

Track a few measures from day one: help desk calls per day, how long people wait, open tickets, and the top ten problems. If the same question keeps coming in, that's a training gap or a build problem to fix at the source. A short proficiency check at the end of training (can the nurse scan a med and chart an assessment in the practice system?) shows who needs more help before go-live.

2e. The math

Training costs paid time: people × hours × cost per hour. Go-live support is counted in shifts: support people per shift × shifts, minus what super users can cover. Both have to fit their budgets.

Practice question (not graded)

420 inpatient nurses each need 12 hours of training, and one hour of a nurse's paid time costs $52. What does their training time cost?

Step 4 · Copilot practice · 60 min

This week's technique: prompt chaining

A big task done in one prompt often comes back shallow or wrong in ways that are hard to spot. Prompt chaining breaks it into a chain of smaller prompts, where each prompt uses the output of the one before. You check each link before you send the next one, so a mistake gets caught early instead of carried all the way through.

Sources: Anthropic, "Prompt engineering best practices for 2026" (published November 10, 2025); Microsoft Learn, "Write prompts that get results" (updated October 2026). You write your own prompts in this course; none are provided to copy.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

Imagine a clinic manager planning flu-shot clinics for 2,000 patients. Instead of one big prompt, she uses three. Prompt 1: "From this patient list, count patients by age group and by clinic." She checks the counts against her spreadsheet before going on; if a clinic is missing, she fixes it now. Prompt 2: "Using these counts, schedule clinics over four Saturdays, no more than 150 shots per nurse per day." She checks the math. Prompt 3: "Using this schedule, list the staff and supplies for each Saturday." An error caught in link 1 never reaches the schedule or the supply list; one found only at the end would be buried three layers deep.
