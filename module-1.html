HCM/MIS 4030 · Healthcare Information Systems Foundations. The lecture is presented by an AI-generated avatar of your instructor. Kestrel Falls Community Hospital and its people are fictional. Real-world facts come from the sources listed at the end.

Welcome to Module 1. This week you start your job at Kestrel Falls Community Hospital as the assistant to Laura Hemmingsen, the Chief of Staff. Laura has asked you for a map of the hospital’s main information systems: what each one does, how it creates value, and what happens if it goes down for a day.

In this video I’ll give you the vocabulary and the big picture you need for that job. We’ll cover what a healthcare information system really is, the clinical systems at the center of care, the business systems that keep the lights on, how systems connect, how they’re protected, where AI fits, and a simple value lens you’ll use all term. Everything I tell you about the real world comes from a named source, and the sources are listed at the end of the transcript.

Let’s start with a definition. A healthcare information system is not just software. It’s people, processes, technology, and data working together so the right information reaches the right person at the right time.

Researchers who study health IT safety make the same point. Dean Sittig and Hardeep Singh, writing in Quality and Safety in Health Care in 2010, described health IT as a sociotechnical system. That means the technology and the social side, the people, their workflows, the organization, and the rules around it, have to be studied and managed together.

Why does that matter to you as a future manager? Because when a system “fails,” the cause is often not the code. It’s a workflow nobody redesigned, a training gap, or a rule that doesn’t fit how people actually work. Keep that in mind when you read Laura’s system list. Each row is a piece of software, but behind each row are people who depend on it.

The electronic health record, the EHR, is the center of the clinical world. It holds notes, orders, results, medication lists, and problem lists. In the United States it’s nearly universal in hospitals. According to the Office of the National Coordinator for Health IT, 99.4 percent of non-federal acute care hospitals used a certified EHR in 2024. Back in 2008, fewer than 10 percent had even a basic EHR. So the question for hospitals today isn’t whether to have an EHR. It’s how well the EHR works, and what happens when it has to change, which is exactly Kestrel Falls Community Hospital’s situation.

Two parts of the EHR matter most to leaders. The first is computerized provider order entry, or CPOE: clinicians enter orders for tests and medicines directly into the system instead of on paper. AHRQ’s Patient Safety Network reports that a 2013 meta-analysis found CPOE cut the likelihood of a prescribing error by 48 percent compared with paper orders. That’s a big safety gain.

The second is clinical decision support: the alerts and reminders built into the system, like a warning that a patient is allergic to the drug being ordered. AHRQ notes that CPOE with built-in decision support reliably prevents prescribing errors. But there’s a catch. Too many alerts, or alerts that don’t fit the situation, cause alert fatigue, where clinicians start clicking past warnings, even important ones. AHRQ calls alert fatigue a recognized safety threat in itself. So more alerts isn’t automatically safer. That’s a management decision, not just a technical one.

Around the EHR sit specialty systems. The imaging archive, called PACS, stores and displays X-rays, CT scans, and MRIs. The laboratory information system tracks specimens and sends results back to the EHR. The pharmacy system lets pharmacists check medication orders and connects to the dispensing cabinets on each unit. Each of these exchanges information with the EHR through interfaces. When an interface breaks, a lab result can sit in one system while the doctor waits in another.

Now the systems that rarely make headlines. Revenue cycle systems turn care into payment: registering the patient, checking insurance, coding the visit, sending the claim, and collecting. ERP, or enterprise resource planning, runs finance, HR, payroll, staff scheduling, and supplies.

If you want proof that these systems matter, look at February 2024. A ransomware attack hit Change Healthcare, a company that processes insurance claims and payments for health care providers. Healthcare Dive reported in January 2025 that UnitedHealth, Change’s parent company, put the number of people whose data was affected at about 190 million. During the outage, providers struggled to get paid, check patients’ coverage, and file prior authorizations, and some practices feared they would have to close. The hospitals themselves weren’t hacked. A system they depended on was. That’s a lesson you’ll use this week: ask who depends on a system, and what breaks for them if it stops.

E-Health means care and health information delivered over the Internet: patient portals, telehealth video visits, and remote monitoring. Telehealth is now common. The CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics found that 37 percent of U.S. adults used telemedicine in the past 12 months in 2021. At Kestrel Falls Community Hospital, the clinics use telehealth mostly for follow-ups and behavioral health.

All of this depends on interoperability: the ability of different systems, often from different vendors, to exchange and use information. ONC measures it in four parts: can a hospital send records, receive them, find them when a patient shows up from somewhere else, and integrate them into its own record? In 2023, 70 percent of hospitals reported doing all four at least sometimes, up from 46 percent in 2018. Large hospitals did better than small ones. So even today, a patient’s records don’t always follow them, and small community hospitals like Kestrel Falls Community Hospital often lag.

Interoperability runs on shared standards. The one you’ll hear most is FHIR, spelled F-H-I-R and pronounced “fire.” ONC describes it as a widely used standard for application programming interfaces, maintained by the standards organization HL7. You don’t need to know how FHIR works. You need to know that when a vendor says it “supports FHIR,” that’s a question about how easily your data can move.

Every system runs on hardware, like servers, workstations, tablets, and medical devices, and on networks: wired, Wi-Fi, and Internet links. The hardware may sit in the hospital’s own data center or in a vendor’s cloud.

Protecting patient information is a legal duty. The HIPAA Security Rule, enforced by the HHS Office for Civil Rights, requires hospitals and their business partners to put administrative, physical, and technical safeguards in place to secure electronic protected health information. Administrative means policies and training. Physical means locked rooms and secured devices. Technical means things like passwords, access controls, and encryption.

And every hospital needs a downtime plan. When systems go down, whether from a power failure, a software bug, or an attack, staff fall back on paper forms and read-only copies. A good manager asks before the crisis: do we have the paper forms, and does everyone know how to use them?

AI is already inside many of these systems. The American Medical Association’s 2026 survey of nearly 1,700 physicians found that 81 percent use AI professionally, more than double the rate when the AMA first asked in 2023. Common uses include summarizing research, drafting discharge instructions and notes, and drafting replies to patient portal messages. One growing use is the ambient scribe, a tool that listens to a visit and drafts the note. You’ll try something like that in Module 5.

The same survey shows the caution. Eighty-eight percent of physicians have at least some concern about losing skills, and most say data privacy and proof of safety are critical before they’ll adopt a tool. That’s the attitude I want you to take into Copilot this term. AI can save time, but it can be wrong. A person stays responsible. The clinician who signs the note owns what it says, and you own every fact in what you hand Laura.

Big system changes are projects. They need a sponsor, a team, a budget, a timeline, and a plan for people, not just technology. That’s what the rest of this course is about, because Kestrel Falls Community Hospital is about to learn that its EHR vendor is leaving the market.

To judge any system, use the value lens you’ll see in your reading. Ask six questions. Operations: does it help daily work run faster or with fewer errors? Strategy: does it help the hospital reach its long-term goals? Finance: does it bring in revenue, cut costs, or protect cash? Quality: does it make care safer or more effective? Patient experience: does it make care easier for patients and families? And organizational performance: does it help leaders measure and improve results?

Let’s try one together. Take the pharmacy system. Operations: pharmacists verify orders quickly, and the dispensing cabinets restock on time. Quality: it catches dosing errors before they reach the patient. Finance: it tracks expensive drugs so they aren’t wasted. If it went down for a day, nurses would wait for medicines and pharmacists would check orders by hand, slower and with more room for error.

That’s the kind of thinking Laura wants in your inventory: who depends on each system, what value it creates, and what breaks if it stops.

2a. What a healthcare information system is

A healthcare information system is more than software. It is people, processes, technology, and data working together so the right information reaches the right person at the right time. When a system fails, the cause is often a process or a person, not the code.

2b. Clinical systems

The electronic health record (EHR) is the center: notes, orders, results, medications, and problem lists. Two parts matter most to leaders: order entry (clinicians enter orders for tests and medicines directly) and clinical decision support (alerts and reminders, such as a drug-allergy warning). Around the EHR sit specialty systems: the imaging archive (PACS), the laboratory information system, and the pharmacy system. Each sends information to and from the EHR through interfaces.

Practice question (not graded)

A nurse orders a medicine in the EHR, and a warning pops up about the patient's drug allergy. Which part of the EHR is doing that?

2c. Administrative and financial systems

Revenue cycle systems turn care into payment: registration, insurance checks, coding, claims, and collections. ERP (enterprise resource planning) systems run finance, HR, payroll, scheduling, and supplies. These systems rarely make headlines, but if they stop, a hospital can run out of cash or supplies.

2d. E-Health, the Internet, and connecting systems

E-Health means care and health information delivered over the Internet: patient portals, telehealth video visits, and remote monitoring. Interoperability is the ability of different systems, often from different vendors, to exchange and use information. It depends on shared standards; a widely used modern one is HL7 FHIR. A health information exchange lets organizations in a region share patient records.

Practice question (not graded)

Which of these is an E-Health tool?

2e. Hardware, networks, and security

Every system runs on hardware (servers, workstations, tablets, medical devices) and networks (wired, Wi-Fi, and Internet links), either in the hospital's own data center or in the cloud. In the United States, HIPAA requires hospitals to protect the privacy and security of patients' health information. Downtime plans matter: when systems go down, staff fall back on paper.

2f. AI in healthcare information systems

AI already appears inside many systems: tools that draft a visit note from the conversation (often called ambient documentation or an ambient scribe), models that flag patients at risk, and chat assistants. AI can save time, but it can be wrong. A person stays responsible: the clinician who signs the note owns what it says.

Practice question (not graded)

An AI tool drafts a doctor's visit note from the conversation in the exam room. Who is responsible for the note being accurate?

2g. Planning, project management, and the future

Big system changes are projects: they need a sponsor, a team, a budget, a timeline, and a plan for people, not just technology. That is what the rest of this course is about. Looking ahead, expect more AI, more care at home, and more data shared across organizations.

This week's technique: zero-shot prompting

A zero-shot prompt is a plain request with no examples and no extra structure: you simply ask. It is how most people start with AI, and it often works for simple tasks. Its weakness is that the AI fills every gap you leave with its own guesses: who the answer is for, how long it should be, and which facts to use. This week you practice asking plainly and then spotting the guesses. In Module 2 you will learn a four-part prompt that closes those gaps.

Source: IBM, "The 2026 Guide to Prompt Engineering" (published July 23, 2025), which names zero-shot prompting. You write your own prompts in this course; none are provided to copy.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

A new clinic manager asked Copilot, with no other detail, to explain what a nurse call system does. The answer was accurate but written for engineers, ran four paragraphs, and described features her clinic does not have. Nothing was "wrong", yet it did not fit her need. She listed what Copilot had guessed (the reader, the length, the features) and added those details to her second request.
