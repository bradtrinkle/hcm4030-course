Welcome to Module 6. The next step in the AMA’s EHR transition guide is deciding how to go live: everyone on one day, which people call a “big bang,” or in phases. Doug has laid out three options, and each one puts a different group at risk. Kestrel Falls Community Hospital’s ten clinics aren’t alike, and the ambulance crews chart in places with no signal. By March 14, Laura needs your rollout recommendation.

In this video I’ll cover the three ways to go live, what real health systems chose and why, the productivity dip, the hidden cost of running two systems at once, keeping the community safe, and how to decide with criteria.

In a big bang, every site switches on the same day. There’s one cutover, no period of running two systems, no temporary interfaces, and everyone learns together. The risk is that every problem hits everywhere at once, support staff are stretched thin, and one bad day affects the whole organization.

In a phased rollout, groups switch in waves weeks or months apart. Each go-live is smaller, early waves teach lessons for later ones, and support can focus. The cost is that old and new systems run side by side, data has to pass between them, and the old system keeps costing money.

A pilot, then big bang, has a small group go first, then everyone else. It finds problems early on a small scale, but the pilot sites carry extra risk, and you still need some temporary connections.

Real health systems choose both ways, and their reasons are instructive. In October 2025, Becker’s Hospital Review reported on several systems moving to a new EHR. Intermountain Health, with 33 hospitals, chose a single go-live. Its digital chief said an enterprise cutover minimizes the period of running parallel systems. Mount Nittany Health, a one-hospital system, also chose a single date. Its chief medical information officer said patient safety is the primary concern, and that a big bang is much less disruptive to patient care than juggling workflows in different systems.

Others went the other way. UPMC, with 40 hospitals and more than 800 outpatient sites, chose phases. Its CIO cited enormous variability in workflows, infrastructure, and readiness, and said a single go-live would carry too high a risk for patient care. Northwell Health, with 21 hospitals, chose a phased, region-based approach to manage readiness and business continuity.

Notice the pattern. Size, how connected the sites are, and the deadline drive the choice. Big bang isn’t automatically reckless, and phased isn’t automatically safe.

Every go-live slows people down while they learn. Hospitals plan for it by booking fewer appointments and cases at first.

In one published example, Brigham and Women’s Hospital in Boston scheduled 20 percent fewer surgical cases during the first two weeks of its EHR switch in 2015. The average time between operations rose from 53 minutes to 63 minutes in the first month. The journal study behind it found the slowdown lasted about five months, and the AMA’s write-up reported that turnover took until the ninth month to fully return to its old level. Either way, the dip lasts months, not days.

Fewer appointments means less revenue and longer waits. So managers have to decide where to cut and who still gets seen first. A clinic with about 24,000 visits a year that books 20 percent fewer for two weeks loses roughly 185 visits. Those are real patients who need a plan.

The hidden cost of a phased rollout is the period when some sites are on the old system and some on the new one. Information still has to flow. A clinic orders a test the hospital runs. A heart patient is admitted from the clinic. Prenatal records have to reach Labor and Delivery.

Each of those flows needs a temporary interface, which has to be built, tested, and then thrown away. Staff who work in both places, like surgeons who see patients in a clinic and operate in the hospital, have to chart in two systems. Each extra month adds risk of missed or duplicated information.

And watch the calendar. If any site is still on Plexmoor after December 31, 2028, Kestrel Falls Community Hospital has to buy Extended Support, which is $3.3 million for 2029 in this case. A rollout’s last wave can quietly change its cost.

A hospital’s go-live affects its whole community. The Joint Commission’s Sentinel Event Alert on the safe use of health IT looks at the sociotechnical side of health IT safety, the same people-plus-technology idea from Module 1, and its suggested actions center on safety culture, process improvement, and leadership. In practice, that means leaders plan downtime procedures, with paper forms and read-only access ready if the system goes down. They tell neighboring hospitals and county emergency services ahead of time. They keep essential services open, like the only urgent care in the county. And they tell patients what to expect, in plain language and in more than one channel.

Rural sites need special checks. A clinic on one slow internet line may struggle with a cloud system on day one. And ambulance crews who chart where there’s no signal need a plan that works offline.

To make a recommendation, compare the options on the same criteria. Patient safety: where are errors most likely? Operations: support staff, training, the productivity dip. Financial: Extended Support, extra support waves, lost visits. Technical: temporary interfaces and connectivity. Legal and ethical: records reaching the right place, and community access to care. And strategic: meeting the Plexmoor deadline, and keeping staff trust.

No option wins on every line. A strong recommendation states its trade-offs openly. If you choose a big bang, say how you’ll protect Walk-in Care and the ambulance crews on day one. If you choose phases, say how you’ll pay for the overlap and keep the temporary interfaces safe.

Let’s practice the method on a made-up case, not Kestrel Falls Community Hospital. Imagine a health system with one hospital and two clinics 15 minutes away, sharing the same doctors. Its old vendor’s support ends in nine months.

Option one, big bang. Patient safety: one cutover, no temporary interfaces, but every unit is learning at once, so it needs heavy at-the-elbow support. Financial: no extended support. Strategic: meets the deadline with room to spare.

Option two, three phases two months apart. Patient safety: smaller go-lives, but the shared doctors chart in two systems for four months, and test orders cross a temporary interface. Financial: the last phase lands after support ends, so the system pays for extended support. Strategic: tight.

On these facts, big bang is easier to defend, because the sites are tightly linked and the deadline is close. But the recommendation still names the trade: the hospital must fund extra support staff for the first two weeks, and it must book fewer appointments while people learn.

Change one fact, say the clinics are three hours away and on their own system, and the answer might flip. That’s the point. Decide from the facts, and show your work.

Step 4 · Copilot practice · 60 min

AI rule for this step: you may use AI (Copilot) to help with your work. Check everything it gives you, and list how you used it in your AI-use disclosure in Step 8.

This week's technique: provide context (why you need it and who is involved)

Copilot can only reason with what you tell it. A bare request ("compare big bang and phased go-lives") gets a textbook answer that could fit any hospital. When you add context, the answer gets specific: why you need it (the decision, the deadline, who will read it), who is involved (the sites, the people, what each depends on), and the limits (dates, budget, staffing). Attaching the source file gives Copilot the facts; your context tells it what matters about them.

Sources: Microsoft Learn, "Write prompts that get results" (updated October 2026); Anthropic, "Prompt engineering best practices for 2026" (published November 10, 2025). You write your own prompts in this course; none are provided to copy.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

A lab director had to choose between moving three hospital labs to a new analyzer system all at once or one lab at a time. Her first request, "pros and cons of all-at-once vs one-at-a-time rollouts," came back generic. She asked again with context: why (a recommendation to her CFO, due Friday, because the old analyzers' service contract ends in June), who is involved (a 24-hour main lab, two small labs open days only, and the ED, which needs results in under an hour), and the limits (four technologists available for training, no budget for overtime). The new answer flagged that the main lab could not close for training and that the ED's turnaround time was the real risk. She checked every fact it used against her notes and found one it had made up (a vendor "recommendation" that was not in her files).
