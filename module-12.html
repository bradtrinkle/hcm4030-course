2a. What a board needs

A hospital board doesn't run the hospital; it decides, oversees, and holds leaders accountable. So a board presentation is not a project report. It answers: What are we being asked to decide? What does it cost? What are the risks? How will we know it's working? Board attention matters. An Institute for Healthcare Improvement review found that more board time spent on quality was associated with better performance on key quality measures, and that lower-performing hospitals often rated their own quality higher than it actually was and were less likely to review their quality dashboard regularly (IHI, Effective Board Governance of Health System Quality, research summary, 2018).

Practice question (not graded)

What belongs on your second slide, right after the title?

2b. Building a board deck

  • One message per slide, written as the slide title ("Big bang meets the deadline without Extended Support"), not a topic ("Rollout").
  • Recommendation first, then the reasons, the cost, the risks, and how you'll know.
  • Numbers that match what the hospital has already said. One wrong total can cost you the room.
  • Honest trade-offs. Name what's hard and what you'll do about it.
  • An appendix for detail the board may ask about. The packet is read without you, so slides must stand on their own.

2c. A measures dashboard for the board

A board dashboard shows a few measures, about 8, not 30. Each one has a target, warning levels (green, yellow, red), an owner, and a frequency. A good set mixes leading measures (they move first, like proficiency-check pass rates or help desk wait times) with lagging ones (they confirm results, like days of cash or nurse turnover), and covers patient safety, staff, finance, patients' experience, and the technology itself. Show trends over time, not just this month's number.

Practice question (not graded)

Which of these is a leading measure for the cash risk after go-live?

2d. Patient safety in the new system

The federal SAFER Guides (Safety Assurance Factors for EHR Resilience) are self-assessment guides that help hospitals check their EHR for the highest-risk, most common safety problems. The 2025 update has eight guides in three groups (foundational, infrastructure, and clinical process), plus a set of High Priority Practices drawn from them (ASTP/ONC, SAFER Guides page, last updated Feb. 27, 2026). Hospitals in Medicare's Promoting Interoperability Program complete a SAFER self-assessment each year, so it makes a natural board safety measure.

Practice question (not graded)

Why might a board want "SAFER High Priority Practices fully in place" on its dashboard?

2e. Feedback after go-live

Go-live is not the finish line. Plan how staff report problems (super users, the command center, huddles, a short survey at 30 and 90 days), how patients report problems (patient relations, the portal, a call line), and how leaders close the loop ("you said, we did"). Clinicians surveyed by KLAS in 2026 asked for accessible, ongoing education and a say in EHR governance after go-live (TechTarget, July 1, 2026). Then decide how and how often the board hears about it.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

A clinic director had to brief her board on a new phone system. She used audience and tone ("for a volunteer board, plain language, five slides") and source file, then verify (she attached her budget sheet). Copilot drafted five slides. Her check found two problems: one slide said the project "will save $80,000 a year," a number that was nowhere in her file, and the cost slide used last year's quote. She fixed both, and she noted in her disclosure that Copilot drafted the slides and she checked every number.
