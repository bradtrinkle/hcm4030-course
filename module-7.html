Welcome back from break, and welcome to Module 7. The next step in the AMA’s EHR transition guide is understanding the current state: how work really gets done today, what connects to what, and how clean the data is. Michele and Sarah Wendt from Health Information Management pulled everything into one workbook. By March 28, Laura needs a current-state assessment with a small dashboard: what matters, the biggest gaps, and what must be cleaned up or rebuilt before go-live.

In this video I’ll cover what a current-state assessment is, counts versus rates, why data quality matters for patients, how to build a simple dashboard, and how to turn findings into decisions.

A current-state assessment looks at three things before a system changes. Workflows: how work actually happens, including the workarounds people invented because the system didn’t fit. Interfaces: every connection that moves data between systems. And data: how complete and accurate the records are.

Leaders need it for two reasons. First, to decide what to rebuild, what to retire, and what to fix. Second, to set a baseline, so that a year from now they can prove the new system made things better, not just different.

Laura put it simply: whatever is messy in Plexmoor will be messy in the new system unless you fix it first. A new EHR doesn’t clean bad data or fix a broken workflow. It copies them faster.

Here’s the most important analytic idea this week. A count tells you how many times something happened. A rate tells you how often it happened compared with how much activity there was.

Imagine two interfaces. One carries thousands of messages a day and logged 300 errors in three months. The other carries a dozen messages a day and logged about 30 errors. If you only look at counts, the busy interface looks like the problem. But divide by the volume, and the quiet one fails far more often per message. The busy one may be quite reliable.

Managers need both. Counts show where the work piles up, like how many tickets the help desk has to handle. Rates show what’s actually broken. In your workbook, compute the rate before you call anything “the worst.” And when you present a rate, say what it’s per: per message, per record, per month.

Common data quality problems in health records include duplicate patient records, which means one person with two charts; blank or outdated allergy and medication lists; and missing contact or language information.

Duplicates sound like a clerical problem. They’re not. In February 2026, BMJ Quality and Safety published a study from 12 hospitals in one U.S. health system. After matching patients carefully, the researchers found that patients with duplicate records were about 3.5 times more likely to need intensive care and almost five times more likely to die in the hospital. It’s an observational study, so it can’t prove that duplicates caused those outcomes. But think about how it could happen: an allergy recorded in one chart and not the other, or a test result that lands in the wrong one.

The researchers note that duplicates are thought to make up about 5 to 10 percent of records. Compare Kestrel Falls Community Hospital’s number to that range in your assessment.

A migration is the best chance to clean data, and the worst time to guess. The federal Office of the National Coordinator’s Health IT Playbook includes migrating your data as its own step, with a chart migration checklist, and it tells organizations to decide how they’ll bring patient histories into the new system. Here’s the trap. With more than 400,000 records, it’s tempting to run an automated tool that merges anything that looks like a duplicate. But an automated merge that’s wrong can join two different people’s records, and that can harm patients at scale. Good plans use automation to find likely duplicates, and trained Health Information Management staff to review and decide. Your recommendation should say who reviews, not just what gets cleaned.

A dashboard is a few charts that answer a leader’s questions at a glance. Four rules make a good one.

Pick the right chart. Bars compare categories, like errors by interface. Lines show change over time, like help desk tickets by month. Pie charts are rarely the best choice, and never for a time trend.

Show the measure that answers the question. Often that’s a rate, not a count.

Use a title that states the finding. “Interface errors” is a label. “Ambulance reports fail most often” is a finding. Laura should get the point even if she reads only the titles.

And add one sentence under each chart saying what it means and what to do. A chart that looks impressive but answers no question wastes the reader’s time. Three clear charts beat ten busy ones.

Each finding should end in an action with an owner. Rebuild this interface and test it first. Retire that one. Clean these records before migration, and name who reviews them. Fix this workflow, rather than copying the workaround into the new system.

Put the findings tied to patient safety first. A failing interface that carries ambulance reports into the emergency department matters more than a slow report that finance runs once a month, even if the finance report has more complaints.

And keep the baseline numbers. When the new system is live, Laura will want to show the board that errors went down and data got cleaner. That’s only possible if you measured before.

Let’s work a made-up example, so you can see the math without me doing your workbook. Interface A carries 1,000 messages a day. In 90 days, that’s 90,000 messages, and it logged 120 errors. Divide 120 by 90,000, and the error rate is about 0.13 percent. Interface B carries 10 messages a day, so 900 messages in 90 days, and it logged 18 errors. Eighteen divided by 900 is 2 percent.

Interface A has more than six times as many errors. Interface B fails about 15 times as often per message. Which one goes on the dashboard as the problem? B, with a title that says so: “Interface B fails 15 times as often as Interface A.” Then one sentence on what it means: if B carries something that matters for patients, rebuild and test it first.

Now a data quality example. A hospital finds 9,000 suspected duplicates in 150,000 records. Nine thousand divided by 150,000 is 6 percent, inside the 5 to 10 percent range the researchers describe. That’s not good news. It’s typical, which means the cleanup still has to happen.

But AI can pick the wrong measure, chart counts when you need rates, or write a confident title the data doesn’t support. Check every number against the workbook, and make sure each title says something true.

2a. What "current state" means

A current-state assessment looks at three things before a system changes: workflows (how work actually happens, including workarounds), interfaces (every connection that moves data between systems), and data (how complete and accurate the records are). Leaders need it for two reasons: to decide what to rebuild, retire, or fix, and to set a baseline so they can later prove the new system made things better.

2b. Counts versus rates

A count tells you how many times something happened; a rate tells you how often, compared with how much activity there was. A busy interface can have many errors and still be reliable; a quiet one can fail often. Managers need both: counts show where the work piles up, and rates show what is broken.

Practice question (not graded)

In the workbook, the bedside monitor interface has 310 errors in 90 days on 5,200 messages a day. The ambulance interface has 31 errors on 12 messages a day. Which statement is right?

2c. Data quality and why it matters for patients

Common data quality problems in health records include duplicate patient records (one person, two charts), blank or outdated allergy and medication lists, and missing contact or language information. Industry estimates put duplicate records at about 5% to 10% of a hospital's records. A 2026 study in BMJ Quality & Safety, covering 12 hospitals in one U.S. health system, found that patients with duplicate records were more likely to need intensive care and to die in the hospital, though the study could not prove the duplicates caused it. A migration is the best chance to clean data, and the worst time to guess: an automated fix that is wrong can harm patients at scale.

Practice question (not graded)

The workbook lists 28,700 suspected duplicates out of 410,000 records. What percent is that, and how does it compare with industry estimates?

2d. Reading and building a simple dashboard

A dashboard is a few charts that answer a leader's questions at a glance. Good ones: pick the right chart (bars to compare categories, lines for change over time), show the measure that answers the question (often a rate, not a count), use clear titles that state the finding ("Ambulance reports fail most often"), and label units. Each chart needs one sentence on what it means and what to do. A chart that looks impressive but answers no question wastes the reader's time.

Practice question (not graded)

Which chart best shows how help desk tickets changed over the last 12 months?

2e. From findings to decisions

Each finding should end in an action with an owner: rebuild this interface and test it first; retire that one; clean these records before migration, and who reviews them; fix this workflow rather than copy the workaround into the new system. Findings tied to patient safety come first.

This week's technique: charts from an Excel file

Copilot can turn a table into a chart when you describe what you want to see. Say which question the chart should answer, which columns to use, the chart type, and how to sort or filter. Then check the chart against the data: AI charts can pick the wrong column, drop rows, or show a count when you needed a rate.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

A dietary services manager had a table of patient meal complaints by unit and month. She asked Copilot for "a chart of complaints." It made a bar chart of total complaints by unit, which mostly showed which units were biggest. She asked again: a bar chart of complaints per 100 meals served by unit, sorted highest first, using the Complaints and Meals columns. The second chart showed a small unit at the top. She checked it by computing that unit's rate by hand, and it matched.
