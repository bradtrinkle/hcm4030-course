Welcome to Module 2. A letter just landed on Doug Thielen’s desk. Plexmoor, the company behind Kestrel Falls Community Hospital’s electronic health record, is leaving the hospital market and will stop supporting the system. Doug sees an IT problem. Laura sees a problem for the whole hospital, and the board will ask her about it on February 16. She wants a one-page executive brief from you by the end of the week.

In this video I’ll explain what “end of support” really means, why running unsupported clinical software is a leadership risk, why executives and not just IT own the decision, the usual options hospitals have, and how to write a brief that a busy executive can act on in two minutes.

Start with a common mistake. When a vendor announces end of support, many people picture the screens going dark on that date. That’s not what happens. The software keeps running. What stops is the vendor’s help: no more bug fixes, no more security patches, no help desk, and no updates when laws or federal requirements change.

The HHS Office for Civil Rights has a name for this. It calls it a legacy system: an information system with components that have been replaced by newer technology and that the manufacturer no longer supports. Every hospital has some legacy technology. The problem at Kestrel Falls Community Hospital is that the legacy system will be the EHR itself, the system almost every clinician uses all day.

So read the notice carefully. It has several dates, and they don’t all mean the same thing. Some services stop earlier than others. Getting those dates exactly right is the first job in your brief.

The biggest risk is security. In that same newsletter, the Office for Civil Rights warns that legacy systems’ lack of vendor support makes them particularly vulnerable to cyberattacks. Attackers look for software that no longer gets patched, because a known weakness stays open forever.

That matters legally too. The HIPAA Security Rule requires hospitals to protect electronic health information with administrative, physical, and technical safeguards. Running software that can’t be patched makes it harder to show you’re doing that.

OCR doesn’t say “never run old software.” It lists options: upgrade to a supported system; contract with the vendor or a third party for extended support, or move to a supported cloud solution; cut the old system off from the internet or the network; or keep it, but add extra protections, which OCR calls compensating controls. And it says organizations should plan for the legacy system’s eventual removal and replacement. In other words, every option except replacement only buys time.

Second, regulation and payment. Medicare’s Promoting Interoperability program asks hospitals to demonstrate meaningful use of certified EHR technology, often shortened to CEHRT. Certification requirements change over time, and the vendor keeps its product certified with updates. When those updates stop, a hospital can fall out of step with federal programs that affect its Medicare payments. Look at the notice again: Plexmoor’s regulatory updates stop six months before the rest of its support. That’s the date that could catch Kestrel Falls Community Hospital off guard.

Third, patient safety. If a bug appears in order entry, or an allergy alert stops firing, and nobody can fix it, patients can be harmed.

Fourth, operations and money. Slow fixes mean downtime. Downtime means paper, delays, and lost revenue. Each month after support ends, the hospital carries more of the risk on its own.

Doug’s instinct to treat this as an IT problem is understandable. It’s software, after all. But replacing an EHR changes how nearly every person in the hospital works: physicians, nurses, pharmacists, coders, registration staff, and patients using the portal.

The American Medical Association’s guide, EHR Transitions: Best Practices, published in 2024, treats an EHR change as a step-by-step organizational project, not a software purchase. It starts with a transition team, buy-in from the people who use the system, and a plan for how to roll it out. You’ll follow those steps for the rest of this course, one module at a time.

So who owns what? Executives own the budget, the timeline, and the people side. IT owns the technical work. Laura is right to take this to the board, and your brief is how she’ll do it.

Hospitals facing end of support usually have four options. Replace the EHR: choose a new vendor and move. Bridge: pay the current vendor for extended support while the replacement is built, which usually costs more and covers less. Third-party support: pay another company to keep the old system running, though it can’t deliver the vendor’s certified regulatory updates, and quality varies. And archive: keep the old records readable after the switch, because patients and clinicians still need the history, and law and policy set how long records must be kept.

Here’s the key idea: these aren’t either-or. Most hospitals combine them. Replace, bridge only if the timeline slips, and archive the old data. Notice how this lines up with the options OCR lists for legacy systems. Only one of them makes the risk go away.

Now the writing. An executive reads your brief in two minutes, often between meetings. So put the bottom line up front. The first two or three sentences say what happened, why it matters, and what you recommend. If Laura reads nothing else, she should still know what to do.

Then follow a simple order. What: the facts, accurate to the letter, with the exact dates. So what: what those facts mean for Kestrel Falls Community Hospital, in terms of security, regulation, patient safety, and money. Now what: the options and the next steps, especially the next 30 days.

Three habits make a brief trustworthy. Keep facts and estimates separate, and label estimates. Say what you don’t know yet; “we have not confirmed the cost of extended support” is useful information. And respect the length. One page means one page.

This week, Copilot can help you draft and tighten the brief. But you are responsible for every date and every number in it. Check each one against the notice.

Laura gave you three questions to ask whenever news like this lands. What exactly did they say? What does it mean for us? What do we do now? Let’s use them on a different, made-up example, so you can see the method without me writing your brief.

Imagine the hospital’s staff scheduling software gets a notice: the vendor will end support for the current version in 14 months and offers an upgrade.

Here’s a weak opening: “Our scheduling vendor, founded in 2004, has sent us a letter about its product roadmap. The letter is attached.” It’s accurate, but Laura has to read the attachment to learn anything.

Here’s a stronger one: “Our scheduling vendor will end support for our current version on March 31 next year. After that date, we get no security patches, so we should approve the upgrade this quarter. The estimated cost is not yet confirmed.” In three sentences, Laura knows what happened, why it matters, what you recommend, and what’s still unknown.

Notice what the strong version does. It uses the exact date. It names the risk in plain words. It recommends an action with a time frame. And it labels the unknown instead of guessing a number.

Then the body follows what, so what, now what. What: the dates and services from the letter, listed exactly. So what: the risks that matter for this hospital, the ones from earlier in this video. Now what: two or three options, and the next steps for the next 30 days, each with an owner.

One last check before you send any brief: read it as Laura would, between two meetings. If the first three sentences don’t tell her what to do, rewrite them.

2a. What "end of support" means

Software does not stop working the day support ends. What stops is the vendor's help: no more bug fixes, no more security patches, no help desk, and no updates when laws or federal requirements change. Every month after that, the hospital carries more of the risk on its own.

2b. Why unsupported clinical software is a leadership risk

  • Security: attackers look for systems that no longer get patches. HIPAA's Security Rule requires hospitals to protect electronic health information; running software that can no longer be patched makes that harder to show.
  • Regulation and payment: federal programs expect hospitals to use certified EHR technology and to report on time. When regulatory updates stop, the hospital can fall out of step. Notice that Plexmoor's regulatory updates stop six months before the rest of its support.
  • Patient safety and quality: a bug in order entry or an alert that no one can fix can harm patients.
  • Operations and finance: slow fixes mean downtime; downtime means paper, delays, and lost revenue.

2e. Writing an executive brief

An executive brief puts the bottom line up front: the first two or three sentences say what happened, why it matters, and what you recommend. Then it follows a simple order: what (the facts), so what (what it means for us), and now what (options and next steps). Keep facts and estimates separate, and say what you do not know yet. This week's technique: Goal, Context, Source, Expectations

In Module 1 you asked Copilot plainly and saw how much it had to guess. Microsoft's prompting guidance for Copilot closes those gaps with four parts. Pay special attention to the Source: give Copilot the actual document, then check every date and number it gives you against that document.

  • Goal: what you want Copilot to do.
  • Context: why you need it and who it is for.
  • Source: the document Copilot must use. Attach the file or paste the text, and tell Copilot to use only that source.
  • Expectations: length, format, and tone.

Source: Microsoft Support, "Get started writing prompts in Microsoft Copilot" (last updated September 2026). You write your own prompts in this course; none are provided to copy.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

A clinic manager received a notice that the company behind the clinic's door-badge system will end support. Her prompt, part by part:

  • Goal: Summarize the attached notice in plain English.
  • Context: It is for the clinic's office staff, who are not technical, so they know what will change for them.
  • Source: Use only the attached notice. If something is not in the notice, say so.
  • Expectations: Five bullet points or fewer, with every date exactly as written in the notice.

Then she checked each date in Copilot's answer against the notice and found one date that Copilot had rounded to the end of the month. She fixed it. That check is the skill.
