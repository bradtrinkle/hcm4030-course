Welcome to Module 9. Three memos landed on Laura’s desk the same morning. Kevin Tran says the move is the most dangerous time for a cyberattack. Ana Morales says cash will dip after go-live. And Randy Dietrich says the budget cut Facilities took to help pay for the EHR could take the new system down on go-live weekend. By April 11, Laura needs a risk register: each risk scored, with an owner, a response, and a warning sign the team can watch.

In this video I’ll cover what a risk register is, why security risk rises during a transition, the cash dip after go-live, the hidden dependencies that can stop a go-live, and how to read warning signs in the numbers.

A risk is something that might happen and would hurt the project if it did. A risk register lists every risk with the same fields, so leaders can compare a cyberattack with a cash shortfall with a failed battery.

Score each risk on two scales from one to five. Likelihood: how likely is it to happen? Impact: how bad would it be for patients, money, or the schedule? Multiply them for a score from one to twenty-five. Scoring likelihood and impact is the standard approach in federal guidance on risk assessment. Then give each risk one owner, a named person, not a committee. Choose a response. Avoid it by changing the plan. Reduce it by making it less likely or less harmful. Transfer it, for example to insurance or a contract. Or accept it, on purpose, with a backup plan. And name a warning sign: a number the owner watches, with a level that triggers action.

A move to a new EHR creates exactly what attackers look for: new accounts, new vendor connections, copies of patient data in test systems, and staff who expect unusual emails like “click here to set up your new login.”

The stakes are high. IBM’s 2025 report found that healthcare breaches cost an average of $7.42 million, more than any other industry, and took about 279 days to identify and contain. And an attack doesn’t have to hit your hospital to hurt it. When the claims company Change Healthcare was attacked in 2024, 94 percent of nearly 1,000 hospitals surveyed by the American Hospital Association reported a financial impact. The Joint Commission’s alert on cyberattacks tells hospitals to be ready to run with life- and safety-critical technology offline for four weeks or longer. That means written downtime plans, trained response teams, and drills people actually practice. Kestrel Falls Community Hospital hasn’t run a drill since 2025.

The second kind of risk is money. After go-live, charges, coding, and claims all run in a new system, with new habits. Things slow down.

Real hospitals have felt it. In 2025, a large cancer center reported a temporary drop in patient activity in the two months after its go-live and an operating loss for the half year, partly from go-live costs. A 25-bed Iowa hospital reported a one-month loss of about $430,000 after its go-live, including about $500,000 of implementation costs. Slower claims are usually delayed money, not lost money. But payroll doesn’t wait. That’s why hospitals watch days of cash on hand: how many days they could pay their bills from cash. In S&P’s preliminary 2025 figures, the median not-for-profit hospital had about 208 days, with an operating margin of about 1.2 percent. Kestrel Falls Community Hospital has about 150.

Software runs on things that aren’t software. Every workstation, scanner, and wireless access point depends on network closets with backup batteries. The servers depend on cooling. And 180 new workstations don’t install themselves.

That’s why Randy’s memo matters. To help fund the EHR, his budget was cut, and the work he’d defer, like generator tests and backup batteries, is exactly what keeps the new system running if the power blinks on go-live weekend.

The lesson is to look past the project’s own budget. A cut in one department can create a risk in another. And the people who were never asked often know where the weak spots are.

Good risk owners watch numbers that move before the harm happens. Those are warning signs, sometimes called leading indicators. A breach report or a missed payroll is a lagging measure. By the time you see it, the harm is done.

In Laura’s measures file, look at the trends, not just the latest number. Vendor remote-access accounts went from 118 to 140 in six months. The coding backlog rose from four days to six. Months since the last downtime drill keep climbing.

For each risk, pick a measure that moves early, then set a trigger: the level at which the owner must act. For example, “if the coding backlog is over five days ninety days before go-live, we add temporary coding help.”

A risk register only works if it’s honest. There’s always pressure to soften bad news, especially before a board meeting. But a board that never hears about a risk can’t fund the fix.

The ACHE Code of Ethics has a section on reporting violations. It says a member who has reasonable grounds to believe another member has violated the Code must report the facts to ACHE’s Ethics Committee. In practice, most problems are best raised first inside the organization, with the person and then with leaders. But hiding a known risk, or reporting that something was done when it wasn’t, isn’t a style choice. It’s an ethics problem.

You can be honest without causing panic. State the risk, the number, the plan, and the date.

Let’s score a made-up risk, not one of Kestrel Falls Community Hospital’s. A clinic is moving its lab into a new building in June. The lab’s refrigerators hold vaccines, and the new building’s backup generator hasn’t been tested.

Likelihood: power outages happen there a few times a year, so a three. Impact: losing a refrigerator of vaccines would cost money and delay care, so a four. Score: twelve.

Owner: the clinic’s facilities manager, by name. Response: reduce. Test the generator under full load before the move, and add a temperature alarm. Warning sign: the generator test result, and the alarm log. Trigger: if the test fails, the move waits.

2a. What a risk register is

A risk is something that might happen and would hurt the project if it did. A risk register lists each one with the same fields, so leaders can compare them:

FieldWhat it means
Likelihood (1 to 5)How likely it is to happen before or soon after go-live. 1 = rare, 5 = almost certain.
Impact (1 to 5)How bad it would be for patients, money, or the schedule. 1 = minor, 5 = severe.
ScoreLikelihood × impact, from 1 to 25. Higher scores get attention first.
OwnerOne named person who watches the risk and acts on it. Not a committee.
ResponseAvoid (change the plan so it can't happen), reduce (make it less likely or less harmful), transfer (shift the cost, for example to insurance or a contract), or accept (live with it, on purpose, with a backup plan).
Warning sign and triggerA measure you can watch, and the level at which the owner must act.

Scoring likelihood and impact is a standard way to assess risk (NIST Special Publication 800-30, Guide for Conducting Risk Assessments, 2012). The score is a judgment, so write down the evidence behind it.

Practice question (not graded)

A risk has likelihood 2 and impact 5. Another has likelihood 4 and impact 3. Which scores higher, and what does that tell you?

2b. Security risk rises during a transition

A move to a new EHR creates extra accounts, vendor connections, copies of data, and unusual emails, which is what attackers look for. Healthcare breaches cost an average of $7.42 million in 2025 and took about 279 days to identify and contain, the longest of any industry (IBM, Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025, as reported July 30, 2025). When the claims company Change Healthcare was attacked in 2024, 94% of nearly 1,000 hospitals in an American Hospital Association survey reported a financial impact (AHA, March 15, 2024). The Joint Commission tells hospitals to be ready to run with key technology offline for four weeks or longer, with downtime plans, trained teams, and practice drills (Sentinel Event Alert 67, August 2023).

Practice question (not graded)

Which of Kevin's risks does a downtime drill reduce?

2c. The cash dip after go-live

Hospitals often feel a money squeeze right after go-live. One large cancer center reported a temporary drop in patient activity in the two months after its February 2025 go-live and an operating loss for the half year, partly from go-live costs (Becker's Hospital Review, Aug. 19, 2025). A 25-bed Iowa hospital reported a one-month loss of $430,135 after its 2025 go-live, including about $500,000 of implementation costs (Becker's, Aug. 14, 2025). Slower claims are usually delayed money, not lost money, but the bills still come due. That is why days of cash on hand matters: the median for not-for-profit acute care providers was about 208 days in S&P's preliminary 2025 figures, with a median operating margin of about 1.2% (Becker's, June 15, 2026).

Practice question (not graded)

KFCH spends about $375 million a year, or about $1.03 million a day. If $29 million arrives late, about how many days of cash does that use up?

2d. Hidden dependencies

Software runs on power, cooling, network closets, and people's hands. A cut to Facilities, a crew too small to install 180 workstations, or a team that was never asked can stop a go-live as surely as a software bug. Good registers look past the project's own budget for these dependencies.

2e. Reading the warning signs

A warning sign (a leading indicator) moves before the harm happens. A lagging measure tells you after. Rising vendor accounts and a growing coding backlog are warning signs; a breach report or a missed payroll is lagging. For each risk, pick a measure that moves early, and a trigger level that makes the owner act.

Practice question (not graded)

In the measures file, the coding backlog went from 4.0 to 6.0 days between October and March. What is the best use of that number?

This week's technique: give permission to say "I don't know"

AI tools try to answer every question, so when the facts run out they may guess and sound sure. You can reduce that by telling Copilot, in your prompt, that it is fine to say it doesn't know: for example, to mark any score or fact the files don't support as "not in the source" or "uncertain," instead of filling the gap. Then you can see where your own judgment is needed.

Sources: Anthropic, "Prompt engineering best practices for 2026" (published November 10, 2025); Microsoft Learn, "Write prompts that get results" (updated October 2026). You write your own prompts in this course; none are provided to copy.

Worked example, on a different made-up case

Imagine a clinic manager who asks an AI tool to list the risks of moving her lab to a new building and score each one. Here is how it could go. The first answer might be a neat table with a confident "likelihood 4" for flooding, even though nothing in her notes mentions water. So she asks again and adds: "Only use my notes. If my notes don't support a score, write 'uncertain' and say what fact you would need." Now scores her notes don't support should come back "uncertain," such as flooding ("need: the building's flood history"). Those are the ones she looks up herself.
